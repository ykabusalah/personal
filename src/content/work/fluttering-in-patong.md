*Content note: this story involves child sex trafficking.*

Flurries of red, blue, and purple strobe lights create hypnotic trances for the people around me; booming stereos and scents of cannabis, alcohol, and lust merge to lull them into a deeper trance. Deep in Patong, the nightlife hub of Phuket Thailand, the relentlessness of its vices is stifling. The underbelly of a nation that I greatly adored was exposed.

I have never enjoyed the “traditional” nightlife. I prefer the quiet ambience of a local coffee shop as my thoughts get drowned out by the conversations of others nearby compared to the booming prison of local clubs. Thailand pushed me to change my course. I had read extensively of the sex tourism industry, which had become a symbol of the nation by design. And with an industry like this, rampant human trafficking. I had never seen a place like this; the closest thing was the outside of a strip club in Houston, Texas on a trip with my friends.

First greeted with the delicious scents of street carts pitched on the side of the road, as fried seafood and fruit sizzle. Families gathered, enjoying dinner and conversing. Laughter. Fireworks going off in the far distance. A calm before the storm. But soon, that smell is drowned out. Not by any particular scent but by sheer depravity. Every corner, foreigners and Thai men alike walked with prostitutes. It reeked of testosterone. Many old men who were 5-6x older than the women they were holding the hands of; eyes mad with lust. The women had fake smiles that would soon evaporate when they turned the other way; even the term “women” is difficult to use as they looked young. Too young.

I began my approach through a mix of English and Google Translate. I was rejected too many times to count. I would pay them double or even triple the rate per hour for an interview at a local coffee shop, explaining my project’s idea and my background. Some of them smiled as they failed to understand my horrible explanation. Others brushed me aside immediately. And many more were terrified and scurried away. There was an air of fear in the eyes of many people I saw that day. A look I know all too well.

In my travels, eyes are usually the key to understanding someone’s true thoughts. In wartime situations, being able to read someone’s eyes can be the difference between life and death. Does the man offering you food have good intentions? Does this man have “that look” that so many refugees possess? Is he a spy instead? Eyes can’t lie. You just need to be able to understand them.

After an hour of going from person to person, it appeared that it wasn’t going to happen. Word had spread about my approaches, and more and more women were opting to completely ignore them. But as I was about to leave the area, Amy tapped me on the shoulder.

“I like an interview,” she said with a look of bright warmth in her eyes. So, after handing her the money in advance, we went and sat down at a local coffee stand in the middle of a bustling market. No recordings. No photos. Just talking.

She was a girl who came from a small village who loved nature and playing with her siblings when she was younger. Sex work came out of necessity; “it was the only way for my family to get out of poverty.” But this path “was never my choice.” Her parents had essentially pushed her to pursue this work to find the funds to support her other siblings. She couldn’t remember the last time she’s seen her parents, and her own memories, outside of moments of play with her siblings, are foggy at best. She’s been in this trade for as long as she can remember. She lives along with the other sex workers, where they’ve formed a bit of a tight-knit group. But despite that, some always leave. She doesn’t really know where they go. All she knows is that every night, she must report to “boss” with her earnings, who then gives her a specific share. Whenever we’d speak about this “boss,” she would be visibly shaken, darting eyes around as if ensuring he’s not around. Reminds me of interrogation time.

Despite this, she harbors a strong hope for the future. A future for an ideal normal life. A life where she is able to go explore coffee shops around the world, marry a nice man and have an adorable family, and see all sorts of butterflies. As she spoke of her future, her eyes lit up for the first time all night; it was the only time that she appeared truly alive. She constantly mentioned the fear that no man would ever want to marry her due to the fact that she was “tainted.”

As she spoke of her life and dreams, one thing bugged me from the moment we sat down. Despite telling me that she was 23 years-old in the beginning, I couldn’t believe it. Despite all the makeup she had put on, I still saw a little girl who resembled my sister in a way. So, as our conversation came to an end, I pushed.

It didn’t take long for her to admit it was a lie. Standard practice. She was 14. She had been trained to avoid this issue and topic, though it wasn’t a major secret. And many knew. Some of her clients were police officers; others even higher-up. I have not felt anger so deep in that moment; the closest was hearing of the martyrs in my local town in Gaza. All you needed was working eyes to see what was right in front of you: a scared, lost child.

My time in Thailand was a lesson in realism. Despite my pleas to help her, whether that be contacting organizations or speaking with law enforcement, she adamantly shut them down. It was a feeling of complete and utter helplessness. So I emptied my pockets of everything I had, gave it to her, and provided my WhatsApp. She never had to reach out, but in the case that anything was possible for me to do, I was there. It’s been about eight months, and I have yet to hear from her.

So many events had to line up for me to meet Amy that day. My conversation with Amy changed the course of my project completely; a project centered solely on refugees became one fixated on displacement.

In our conversation, Amy constantly spoke of her love of butterflies. From the way they flew so freely to the beautiful colors they had, it was one of the few times that she shed a genuine smile. Her favorite were the TODO: butterfly species native to Thailand. So, I decided to represent her through those butterflies. I am unsure if I will ever see her again, but that conversation lives with me every day.

> “I’ve loved butterflies my whole life. I’ve loved them ever since I was a child.”
