*Content note: this story mentions suicide and cancer.*

Heraklion had become a second home for me. Greece, in general, had always been a dream. The birthplace of democracy. The inspiration for the country that not only housed me but had become a safe, stable, and proud home. And while Athens took my breath away, Heraklion, with its dirtier areas, felt more like an organic and cozy home. The souvlakis grilling in the stands on the streets. The cobblestone streets. It felt like Jordan and Palestine in many ways.

I had just visited the Minoan ruins, taking a bus back to my home near the city center. An old woman trudged past me, her hijab lightly covering the wisps of hair begging to escape. She was around 5’1 with disheveled clothes and an obvious limp, with black earbuds protruding from her pockets. As she trudged to her seat, the bus makes a sudden stop, shaking her and catapulting her phone to the ground. And with that, a familiar tune begins to play out into the empty bus: Tamer Hosny. A particular favorite artist of my childhood and an indication that this woman was Arab.

In Greece, I had been mistaken for Greek many times, so much so that I started creating a false genealogy for myself. My father was from Heraklion and my mother from Kefalonia. This would be a trend that would continue on in many countries that I went to. So this woman, I assumed, was Greek like myself. But after hearing this tune, I quickly approached her with Arabic, which was followed with a bright and beautiful glow from her eyes. Success.

We began to speak, little by little. She spoke of the weird accent my Arabic had (“Are you from the Duffa?” Palestine). We exchanged names, nationalities, favorite dishes, tribe names, etc. She was from Syria. I from Palestine. We both left our homelands when things got too hard. One as a child supported by his family. Another as a widow alongside her small son. She now was homeless in the street, as her son had committed suicide the day before. She asked if she could feel my hair, as I seemed to have a particular resemblance to her son. I obliged. She smelled of home. She spoke like my grandma, who I hadn’t seen for seven years. She took me back to times sitting in an old-style restaurant in Amman, Jordan, as Umm-Kulthum blared on the speakers among the tapestries and antiques and the scents of apple, molasses, and tobacco argeeleh dissipated into the air. I found divinity in her honey-colored eyes.

And as I settled to the most peace I had felt since I had arrived in America, she mentioned her cancer diagnosis. Breast cancer. Stage four. And in that, my peace was shattered. In front of me lay a woman on her deathbed. A woman who represented home. And more specifically, a woman who reminded me of what could’ve been.

In my senior year of college, my mother was diagnosed with breast cancer. It completely shook me. It led to fallen grades and a desperate push to raise funds to pay for the treatment, which my family couldn’t have ever begun to pay for on our own.

In this woman, I saw what my mother could’ve been. What could’ve happened had she not escaped Kuwait during the war. What could’ve happened had my family not escaped to the US. What could’ve happened if many friends, family, and people from across the globe hadn’t given the money for the treatment when they did.

I cried. I cried and cried and cried. For the first time, the emotions were too much for me. I couldn’t bear them. In between coughs and wailing, I lost myself in grief; I lost myself in guilt. Survivor’s guilt. Financial guilt. I didn’t know what to do. Nothing felt right. And she proceeded to put my head on her shoulder. We stayed like that for a while. Even as other people boarded the bus and gave side-glances, she caressed my head and whispered “it’s okay habibi.”

I don’t know how many stops we passed. I don’t know how long we stayed on that bus.

Fatima never changed her loving expression. Never pushed me away. Never moved me or pressured me to get up. She accepted my pain, an absolute stranger, with love, patience, and care.

After my tears dried, she looked me in the eyes. “You are so much like my son, Osama. You have his eyes. The way you dart around while you talk. In this time, it feels like I’m speaking with him again. Thank you.”

Me and Fatima talked for the rest of the night, about everything. About my worries for marriage. About what gives her strength. Political discussions. Introductions to anime. What she thought about these weird Arab remixes with rap songs I found on YouTube.

But eventually, she wanted to sleep. I asked her if she wanted to use my AirBnb, and I could go to a local hotel. She refused. I attempted to give her all the money I had in my wallet. She refused. And I finally gave her my WhatsApp # to keep in contact for the future. She accepted.

“Thank you for giving me the opportunity to talk with my son again.”

I sent her the drawing I made of our time together about two weeks after our conversation. And I periodically send updates and questions to her phone every Friday. But after the first few replies, they all stopped. It’s been about seven months, and I still haven’t heard from her. I hope she’s gotten a new phone. Maybe she’s even gotten medical help and she’s in a new country now.

My eyes have dried up at this point. But her bright honey-colored eyes, wisps of beautiful gray hair, and toothy but cheery smile will never leave my mind. There are people in this life who shine brighter than any stars. They exude an aura that attracts life, happiness, and comfort. I lost that shine long ago, but Fatima reminded me of what it could look like. I am eternally thankful for that.
